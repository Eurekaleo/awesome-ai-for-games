Foundation Models for Game World and Player Modeling

How learned models represent game dynamics, generate interactive worlds, support planning, and predict player behavior.

Direct answer

Foundation models and world models for games learn or generate representations of game dynamics, state transitions, environments, and player behavior. A world model may predict the next state for planning, provide a learned environment for policy training, simulate possible futures, or generate an interactive experience directly from actions and prior observations. Video-based models can produce visually convincing frames, while structured or latent models may focus on mechanics, controllable state, or decision-relevant features. These goals require different evidence. Visual realism does not prove that collisions, inventory, health, rules, or long-term consequences are correct, and short clips do not establish persistent state across revisits. Models of players address a related but distinct problem: forecasting choices, preferences, skill, affect, or trajectories from behavioral data. Such predictions can support analytics or adaptation, but they do not by themselves define what a game should change. Evaluation should therefore match the model's consumer—planning, training, simulation, generation, or player analysis—and test perceptual quality, mechanics fidelity, state persistence, uncertainty, policy transfer, and performance on held-out players or games.

Evidence

What should evaluation establish?

World-model quality is task dependent. Evaluations should separate appearance from mechanics and persistent state, then show that the representation helps the intended downstream consumer rather than relying on a single visual-quality score.

  • Perceptual and temporal quality
  • Mechanics and rule consistency
  • Persistent state over long interaction
  • Policy transfer or forecast accuracy
